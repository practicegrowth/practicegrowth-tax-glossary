# Texas Franchise Tax

## Overview

The **Texas Franchise Tax** is a state-level tax on businesses operating in Texas. It's commonly — and misleadingly — called the "Texas margin tax." Despite Texas having no personal income tax, most business entities with gross receipts above $2.47 million (2024–2025 reporting threshold) must file and potentially pay this tax annually.

**Key fact:** Texas taxes businesses on their *gross receipts* (with deductions) rather than net income. This means you can owe tax even if your business operates at a loss. For Dallas-Fort Worth businesses in particular — home to one of the highest concentrations of small and mid-market companies in the US — franchise tax compliance is a recurring annual obligation that catches many first-time entrepreneurs off guard.

## Who Must File

The franchise tax applies to most entities formed or doing business in Texas, including:

| Entity Type | Subject to Franchise Tax? |
|---|---|
| Corporations (C-Corp, S-Corp) | Yes |
| LLCs | Yes |
| Limited Partnerships (LP) | Yes |
| Professional Associations (PA) | Yes |
| Business Trusts | Yes |
| Sole Proprietorships (individuals) | No |
| General Partnerships wholly owned by individuals | No |
| Certain passive entities | May qualify for exemption (review criteria carefully) |

## The Math: How the Tax Is Calculated

**Texas Franchise Tax = Taxable Margin × Tax Rate**

### Step 1: Calculate Total Revenue
Total revenue reported on your federal tax return, with certain statutory exclusions (flow-through funds, certain pass-through revenues from affiliated entities).

### Step 2: Choose Your Deduction Method
Deduct from total revenue **whichever is larger:**

| Deduction Option | Calculation | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| **30% of total revenue** | Total Revenue × 70% = Taxable Margin | Most service businesses (CPAs, lawyers, consultants) |
| **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** | Total Revenue − COGS = Taxable Margin | Retailers, manufacturers, wholesalers |
| **Compensation** | Total Revenue − Compensation (capped at $400K/person) = Taxable Margin | Labor-intensive businesses with high payroll |
| **EZ Computation** | Total Revenue × 80% (available only if total revenue ≤ $20M) | Small businesses wanting simplicity |

### Step 3: Apply the Rate

| Taxpayer Type | Rate |
|---|---|
| Retail and wholesale | 0.375% |
| Most other businesses (including services) | 0.75% |

### Step 4: Check the No Tax Due Threshold
If your **total revenue** is ≤ $2,470,000 (2024–2025 threshold), you owe no tax — but you must still file a No Tax Due Report (Form 05-163). The threshold is adjusted periodically; check the Texas Comptroller's website for the current year's amount.

---

## Quick Example

A CPA firm in Dallas with $500,000 in gross revenue:
- 30% deduction method: $500,000 × 70% = $350,000 taxable margin
- Tax: $350,000 × 0.75% = **$2,625**
- Must file Form 05-163 by May 15

If the same firm had only $200,000 in revenue, it falls under the $2.47M no-tax-due threshold — no tax owed, but the No Tax Due Report is still required.

## Common Situations for Texas Businesses

### New Business Formation
A new LLC formed in Texas in March must determine if it qualifies for the first-year exemption or needs to file within 90 days. Most small startups fall below the $2.47M threshold in their first year and file the No Tax Due Report — but missing this filing can result in forfeiture of the right to transact business in Texas.

### Multi-State Operations
Businesses operating in Texas AND other states must apportion their margin. Texas uses a single-factor (gross receipts) apportionment formula: only the portion of revenue sourced to Texas is subject to the franchise tax. For DFW businesses with multi-state clients, correct sourcing is critical.

### DFW-Area Professional Services
Dallas-Fort Worth has one of the nation's highest concentrations of professional service firms — CPAs, law firms, consulting practices, and architecture firms. These businesses typically use the 30% deduction method (limited COGS eligibility), making franchise tax a straightforward calculation but a meaningful annual expense at higher revenue levels.

---

## Can I avoid the Texas Franchise Tax entirely by forming my LLC in another state?

No. The franchise tax applies to entities "doing business" in Texas, regardless of where they're formed. If your Delaware LLC has operations, employees, or revenue sourced to Texas, you're subject to the tax. The legal test is nexus — physical presence, economic presence, or having a Texas-based customer base can all trigger filing obligations.

---

## What happens if I don't file the No Tax Due Report?

Even if you owe zero tax, failing to file the No Tax Due Report can result in your entity losing its right to transact business in Texas. The Comptroller can also issue a notice of forfeiture, which has cascading effects — your LLC may lose the ability to sue or defend itself in Texas courts, and personal liability protections may be compromised until the filing is brought current.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Texas has no income tax, so my LLC owes nothing" | Texas has no *personal* income tax. The franchise tax applies to most business entities regardless of the owner's personal state tax situation. |
| "I can use COGS as a service business" | COGS is narrowly defined in Texas. Service businesses — CPAs, law firms, consulting practices — generally cannot use COGS and must default to the 30% or compensation deduction. |
| "If I owe no tax, I don't need to file anything" | The No Tax Due Report (Form 05-163) is mandatory. Non-filers face forfeiture of their right to do business in Texas, even with zero tax liability. |
| "The EZ computation is always simplest and best" | The EZ method caps taxable margin at 80% of revenue but uses different effective rates. For many businesses, the standard 30% deduction yields lower tax. Calculate both. |
