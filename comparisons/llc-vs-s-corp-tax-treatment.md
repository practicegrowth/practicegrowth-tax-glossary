# LLC vs S-Corporation: Tax Treatment Comparison

> A PracticeGrowth Tax Comparison — from the [PracticeGrowth Tax Glossary](https://www.PracticeGrowth.tech/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=comparisons)

**Published:** August 2026 · **Last reviewed:** August 2026

---

## The Short Answer

An **LLC** is a legal entity type, not a tax classification. An **S-Corporation** is a tax election — and an LLC can elect S-Corp treatment. The key difference: **S-Corp owners split income into salary (payroll tax applies) and distributions (no self-employment tax), potentially saving $10,000+ per year at $150,000+ profit levels.**

The real comparison is between default LLC taxation (sole proprietorship or partnership, where all profit is subject to 15.3% self-employment tax) and S-Corp taxation (where only salary is subject to payroll tax).

---

## Side-by-Side

| | LLC (Default Tax Treatment) | S-Corporation |
|---|---|---|
| **Taxed as** | Sole proprietorship (Schedule C) or partnership (Form 1065) | Pass-through corporation (Form 1120-S) |
| **Self-employment / payroll tax** | All business profit subject to 15.3% self-employment tax | Only salary subject to FICA; distributions are exempt |
| **Reasonable salary** | Not required | Required — owner-employees must take W-2 wages at "reasonable compensation" |
| **Payroll complexity** | None — just quarterly estimated tax payments | Must run payroll, file 941/940, issue W-2 to owner |
| **Profit distribution** | All profit passes through to owner's personal return | Distributions beyond salary not subject to FICA or self-employment tax |
| **Filing requirements** | Schedule C (1 member) or Form 1065 + K-1s (2+ members) | Form 1120-S + Schedule K-1s, due March 15 |
| **Ownership limits** | No restrictions | Max 100 shareholders, all US citizens/residents, one class of stock only |
| **Best for** | Businesses under ~$70K profit, side businesses, owner IS the service | Businesses with profit well above the owner's reasonable salary |

## Decision Framework

### Stay with default LLC taxation if:
- Your net profit is under ~$70,000 (the FICA savings may not cover the added payroll and accounting costs)
- You're the sole revenue generator and your income IS essentially all your labor
- You want minimal paperwork — no payroll, no separate corporate return
- You're testing a business idea and want to keep compliance simple

### Elect S-Corp taxation if:
- Your business consistently profits above $70,000–$100,000
- You can justify a reasonable salary meaningfully lower than total profit
- You're willing to run payroll and file a separate corporate return
- You expect the business to grow and want the structure in place early
- You have employees and already run payroll infrastructure

### The Math: What S-Corp Saves You

| Business Profit | Reasonable Salary | Distribution (S-Corp) | Self-Employment Tax Savings |
|---|---|---|---|
| $100,000 | $60,000 | $40,000 | ~$6,120 |
| $150,000 | $80,000 | $70,000 | ~$10,710 |
| $200,000 | $100,000 | $100,000 | ~$15,300 |
| $300,000 | $120,000 | $180,000 | ~$17,774* |

*\*Above the Social Security wage base ($168,600 for 2025), only Medicare tax (2.9%) applies to additional salary, reducing marginal savings.*

## Timing: When to Make the S-Corp Election

This is the #1 question CPAs get about S-Corps, and the deadlines are unforgiving.

- **Existing entities:** File **Form 2553** by **March 15** of the tax year you want S-Corp status to begin.
- **New entities:** File within **75 days** of formation (earliest of: date of incorporation, date the business begins, or date it issues stock).
- **Late election relief:** IRS Revenue Procedure 2013-30 provides automatic relief for late elections filed within 3 years and 75 days of the intended effective date, provided the entity intended to be classified as an S-Corp and has reasonable cause for the late filing.
- **Once revoked:** If you revoke S-Corp status, you generally must wait **5 years** before re-electing.

**Missed the deadline? Talk to a CPA immediately.** Late relief is available but not guaranteed.

## Can I set my S-Corp salary at $1 to avoid all payroll tax?

No. The IRS requires "reasonable compensation" for S-Corp owner-employees who perform services for the business. Taking an unreasonably low salary is one of the most common S-Corp audit triggers. What's "reasonable" depends on your industry, role, hours, experience, and what you'd pay someone else to do your job. If the IRS reclassifies distributions as wages, you'll owe back FICA taxes, penalties, and interest.

---

## Does an S-Corp protect me from all taxes?

No. The S-Corp is a pass-through entity — you still pay income tax on ALL profit at your individual rate. The tax savings come only from avoiding self-employment tax (15.3%) on distributions. You still pay income tax on distributions — just not the FICA/SE portion.

---

## How does the QBI deduction affect the LLC vs S-Corp decision?

The **Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction** allows a 20% deduction on qualified business income. For S-Corp owners, QBI is calculated on business profit *after* deducting the owner's salary — which reduces the QBI deduction compared to a sole proprietorship at the same profit level. This partially offsets the SE tax savings. This is why the decision requires running actual numbers, not rules of thumb. The QBI trade-off matters most in the $100K–$200K profit range.
