# Standard Deduction vs Itemized Deductions

> A PracticeGrowth Tax Comparison — from the [PracticeGrowth Tax Glossary](https://www.PracticeGrowth.tech/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=comparisons)

**Published:** August 2026 · **Last reviewed:** August 2026

## The Short Answer

The **standard deduction** is a fixed dollar amount you subtract from your income — no questions asked, no receipts needed. **Itemized deductions** are specific expenses you track and claim individually. You pick whichever gives you the larger reduction. For 2025, the standard deduction is $15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married filing jointly.

Most taxpayers use the standard deduction (~90% since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act nearly doubled it). But if your qualifying expenses exceed the standard amount, itemizing saves you more.

---

## Side-by-Side

| | Standard Deduction | Itemized Deductions |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Fixed amount set by IRS, based on filing status, age, and blindness | List of eligible expenses you track and report on Schedule A |
| **Paperwork** | None required | Must keep receipts, records, and documentation |
| **Common items** | Automatic — nothing to prove | Mortgage interest, state/local taxes (SALT cap $10K), charitable donations, medical expenses > 7.5% AGI |
| **2025 amounts (est.)** | $15,000 Single / $30,000 MFJ / $22,500 HOH | Varies — total of all eligible expenses |
| **Best for** | Renters, those with few deductible expenses, simplicity | Homeowners with mortgages, high charitable givers, high medical expenses |

## Decision Framework

### Use the standard deduction if:
- You rent your home (no mortgage interest to deduct)
- Your state and local taxes are well under the $10,000 SALT cap
- You don't make large charitable contributions
- You value simplicity and don't want to track receipts
- Your total itemizable expenses are clearly below the standard amount

### Itemize if:
- You paid significant mortgage interest during the year
- You made large charitable contributions (cash or non-cash)
- You had major unreimbursed medical expenses above 7.5% of AGI
- Your state/local taxes plus property taxes approach or exceed the $10K SALT cap
- You had casualty or theft losses from a federally declared disaster

### The "Bunching" Strategy
If your itemizable expenses are just under the standard deduction threshold, consider **bunching** — grouping two years of charitable contributions into one tax year so you can itemize in the bunched year and take the standard deduction in the off year. A donor-advised fund (DAF) is a common vehicle for executing this strategy.

---

## Can I take both the standard deduction and itemize in the same year?

No. You must choose one. The IRS does not allow a taxpayer to claim the standard deduction AND itemized deductions on the same return. You file Schedule A only if itemizing.

---

## Is itemizing always better than taking the standard deduction?

Not necessarily. Only if your total eligible expenses exceed the standard deduction for your filing status. Many taxpayers who itemized before 2018 no longer benefit after the TCJA nearly doubled the standard deduction. Before deciding, total up your mortgage interest, state and local taxes (capped at $10,000), charitable gifts, and medical expenses above 7.5% of AGI.

---

## Can I deduct all of my state and local taxes if I itemize?

No. The SALT deduction is capped at $10,000 ($5,000 if married filing separately). This includes state income tax OR sales tax, plus property tax — combined. If you pay $15,000 in property tax and $8,000 in state income tax, you can only deduct $10,000 total.

---

## Are all my medical expenses deductible if I itemize?

No. Only the portion of unreimbursed medical and dental expenses that exceeds 7.5% of your Adjusted Gross Income is deductible. For example, if your AGI is $100,000 and you had $12,000 in medical expenses, you can deduct $4,500 ($12,000 minus $7,500).

---

## Can I alternate between standard and itemized deductions each year?

Yes, you can switch year to year. This is exactly what the bunching strategy exploits. There's no lock-in — each tax year stands alone, and you choose whichever method produces the lower tax liability.
