# Traditional IRA vs Roth IRA

> A PracticeGrowth Tax Comparison — from the [PracticeGrowth Tax Glossary](https://www.PracticeGrowth.tech/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=comparisons)

**Published:** August 2026 · **Last reviewed:** August 2026

## The Short Answer

A **Traditional IRA** gives you a tax break today — contributions may be deductible, growth is tax-deferred, and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income in retirement. A **Roth IRA** gives you a tax break tomorrow — contributions are after-tax, but qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free, and there are no Required Minimum Distributions during your lifetime. For 2025, the contribution limit is $7,000 ($8,000 if age 50+).

The deciding factor is usually your expected tax bracket in retirement vs. now. If you expect to be in a lower bracket later, Traditional wins. If you expect the same or higher, Roth wins.

---

## Side-by-Side

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Tax treatment of contributions** | May be deductible (phases out if covered by a workplace plan and income exceeds limits) | Not deductible — contributed after-tax |
| **Tax treatment of growth** | Tax-deferred (no tax until withdrawal) | Tax-free (qualified withdrawals pay zero tax) |
| **Tax treatment of withdrawals** | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free if qualified: age 59½ + 5-year rule |
| **Required Minimum Distributions** | Must begin at age 73 (age 75 starting 2033) | No RMDs during owner's lifetime |
| **Income limits — contributions (2025)** | No limit to contribute, but deduction phases out: $79K–$99K Single / $126K–$146K MFJ (if covered by workplace plan) | Contribution phases out: $150K–$165K Single / $236K–$246K MFJ |
| **Contribution limit (2025)** | $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+) — combined across all IRAs | Same — the $7,000/$8,000 limit applies to combined Traditional + Roth contributions |
| **Early withdrawal** | 10% penalty before 59½ (with exceptions: first home, education, medical, etc.) | Contributions can be withdrawn anytime tax/penalty-free; earnings face 10% penalty + income tax before 59½ |
| **Best for** | High earners who expect lower bracket in retirement; those who need the upfront deduction | Younger earners in low brackets; those expecting higher income later; estate planning |

## Decision Framework

### Choose Traditional IRA if:
- You're in a high tax bracket now (24%+) and expect a lower bracket in retirement
- You need the upfront deduction to reduce your current-year tax bill
- Your income is below the deduction phase-out AND you're covered by a workplace plan
- You're late in your career with a shorter tax-deferred growth window

### Choose Roth IRA if:
- You're early in your career and in a lower bracket than you expect later
- You want tax-free income in retirement and the flexibility of no RMDs
- You value the ability to withdraw contributions (not earnings) before 59½ without penalty
- You're doing estate planning — Roth IRAs pass to heirs income-tax-free
- Your income exceeds the Traditional IRA deduction phase-out range

### Income Scenario Quick Reference

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| 25-year-old, $45K income, no workplace plan | Roth IRA — pay low tax now, decades of tax-free growth |
| 35-year-old, $200K income, maxing 401(k) | Backdoor Roth IRA — too high for direct Roth or deductible Traditional |
| 45-year-old, $150K income, has 401(k) | Traditional IRA if deductible, plus Roth for tax diversification |
| 55-year-old, $90K income, retiring in 10 years | Traditional IRA — reduce taxes now; plan Roth conversions in low-income retirement years |
| Self-employed, variable income | Roth in low-income years, Traditional in high-income years |

## How does the 5-year rule work for Roth IRAs?

For Roth IRA **earnings** to be withdrawn tax-free, two conditions must be met: you must be at least age 59½ AND your first Roth IRA contribution must have been made at least 5 tax years ago. The 5-year clock starts on January 1 of the tax year you made your first contribution to ANY Roth IRA. **Contributions** can be withdrawn at any time, tax-free and penalty-free, regardless of age or how long the account has been open.

---

## What is a Backdoor Roth IRA?

If your income exceeds the Roth IRA contribution limits ($165K single / $246K MFJ for 2025), you can contribute to a non-deductible Traditional IRA and then **convert** it to a Roth IRA. There's no income limit on Roth conversions. However, if you have existing pre-tax Traditional IRA balances, the **pro-rata rule** applies — you can't convert just the non-deductible portion. The IRS treats the conversion as coming proportionally from all your Traditional IRA funds, potentially creating an unexpected tax bill. This is why the backdoor Roth works best when you have zero existing Traditional IRA balances.

---

## Can I contribute to both a Traditional and Roth IRA in the same year?

Yes. The annual contribution limit ($7,000/$8,000 for 2025) is the **combined total** across all your IRAs. You can split the contribution between Traditional and Roth however you want — for example, $3,500 to each. But you cannot exceed the combined limit.

---

## Are Roth IRAs always better because of tax-free growth?

Not always. The comparison depends entirely on your tax rate now vs. your tax rate in retirement. If you're in the 32% bracket today and expect to be in the 12% bracket in retirement, Traditional's upfront deduction is worth far more than Roth's tax-free growth. The math: $7,000 in a Traditional IRA at 32% saves $2,240 in current taxes, which can be invested separately. Run the numbers for your specific situation — don't rely on rules of thumb.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Roth is always better because tax-free growth sounds better" | The comparison is tax rate now vs. tax rate later. If you're in a high bracket now, Traditional's deduction is more valuable. |
| "I make too much to contribute to any IRA" | There are no income limits for non-deductible Traditional IRA contributions. You can always contribute; the question is whether it's deductible. |
| "I can withdraw Roth earnings tax-free at any time" | Only **contributions** can be withdrawn penalty-free at any time. **Earnings** face the 10% penalty plus income tax before age 59½, unless a qualified exception applies. |
| "I have to pick one type and stick with it forever" | You can contribute to both types in the same year, split however you want, as long as the combined total doesn't exceed the annual limit. |
