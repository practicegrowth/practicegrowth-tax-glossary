# Accrual vs Cash Accounting

> A PracticeGrowth Tax Comparison — from the [PracticeGrowth Tax Glossary](https://www.PracticeGrowth.tech/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=comparisons)

**Published:** August 2026 · **Last reviewed:** August 2026

## The Short Answer

**Cash accounting** records income when you receive payment and expenses when you pay them. **Accrual accounting** records income when earned (invoiced) and expenses when incurred (billed), regardless of when cash moves. For 2025, businesses with average annual gross receipts under $30 million can generally choose either method.

Most small businesses and sole proprietors use cash accounting. Businesses with inventory, C-corporations above the threshold, and publicly traded companies are generally required to use accrual.

---

## Side-by-Side

| | Cash Method | Accrual Method |
|---|---|---|
| **Income recorded** | When payment is received | When invoice is sent (earned) |
| **Expenses recorded** | When bill is paid | When bill is received (incurred) |
| **Tax timing flexibility** | Can defer income by delaying December billing to January | Income taxed in the year earned, even if unpaid |
| **Complexity** | Simple — matches bank statements | More complex — requires tracking receivables and payables |
| **Cash flow visibility** | Shows actual cash on hand | Shows earned revenue and outstanding obligations |
| **IRS eligibility (2025)** | Gross receipts < $30M avg (3-year test) | Required above $30M or for inventory-heavy businesses |
| **Best for** | Small service businesses, freelancers, sole proprietors | Businesses with inventory, growing companies, those seeking financing |

## Decision Framework

### Use cash accounting if:
- Your business is a sole proprietorship or small LLC without inventory
- Your average annual gross receipts are under $30 million (Section 448)
- You want simplicity — your books match your bank account
- You benefit from the ability to time income and expenses across tax years
- You provide services rather than selling physical products

### Use accrual accounting if:
- Your business carries inventory that is a material income-producing factor
- Your average annual gross receipts exceed $30 million
- You need GAAP-compliant financial statements for lenders or investors
- You want a more accurate picture of long-term profitability
- You're a C-corporation above the threshold or a partnership with a C-corp partner

### Hybrid Accounting
The IRS allows qualified small businesses to use a **hybrid method** — accrual for inventory and cash for everything else. This preserves simplicity where possible while maintaining compliance on inventory reporting. If you sell products AND services, this is often the optimal setup.

---

## Can I switch between cash and accrual accounting whenever I want?

No. Changing your accounting method requires filing **Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method)** with the IRS. You cannot flip back and forth to optimize taxes year to year. The IRS must approve the change, and switching may trigger a **Section 481(a) adjustment** — a one-time catch-up that can create a significant tax bill if you have large outstanding receivables.

---

## Does cash accounting mean I don't pay tax on unpaid invoices?

Correct — but only while you remain on the cash method. If you invoice a client $50,000 in December but don't receive payment until January, that $50,000 is taxed in January's tax year under cash accounting. However, if you later switch to accrual, those outstanding receivables may be caught up through the Section 481 adjustment.

---

## Do all small businesses qualify for cash accounting?

Not automatically. Businesses with inventory must generally use accrual for inventory accounting, even if they use cash for other items. However, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act expanded cash-method eligibility — if your average gross receipts are under $30 million, you may qualify even with inventory. Check Section 448(c) and consult a tax professional.

---

## Can I prepay expenses in December to reduce this year's taxable income?

Yes — with limits. Under the **12-month rule**, you can deduct prepaid expenses if the benefit does not extend beyond the earlier of 12 months or the end of the next tax year. Prepaying a 3-year insurance policy in December does NOT qualify for a full current-year deduction; only the portion covering the next 12 months is deductible.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Reality |
|---|---|
| "I can change methods whenever it helps my taxes" | You need IRS approval via Form 3115. Frequent changes are a red flag for audit. |
| "Cash method means I never pay tax on unpaid work" | Income is deferred, not eliminated. When you receive payment, it's taxable. Plus, switching methods can trigger a catch-up adjustment. |
| "Accrual always means higher taxes" | Accrual can accelerate expense recognition — you deduct bills when incurred, before paying them. This may lower taxable income in some years. |
| "All small businesses can use cash without restriction" | Inventory-based businesses have accrual requirements regardless of size, unless they qualify under the $30M gross receipts exception. |
