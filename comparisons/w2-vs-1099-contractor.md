# W-2 Employee vs 1099 Independent Contractor

> A PracticeGrowth Tax Comparison — from the [PracticeGrowth Tax Glossary](https://www.PracticeGrowth.tech/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=glossary&utm_campaign=comparisons)

**Published:** August 2026 · **Last reviewed:** August 2026

## The Short Answer

A **W-2 employee** works for an employer who withholds taxes, pays half the payroll taxes, and controls how the work is done. A **1099 independent contractor** is self-employed — paying the full 15.3% self-employment tax, handling their own withholding, and controlling their own work. The IRS determines your classification based on behavioral control, financial control, and the relationship — not what your contract says.

Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can cost an employer back taxes, penalties, and the employee's share of FICA.

---

## Side-by-Side

| | W-2 Employee | 1099 Independent Contractor |
|---|---|---|
| **Who pays FICA** | Employer 7.65% + employee 7.65% | Contractor pays full 15.3% (but half is deductible above the line) |
| **Tax withholding** | Employer withholds income tax, Social Security, Medicare | Contractor makes quarterly estimated tax payments (Form 1040-ES) |
| **Benefits** | May receive health insurance, 401(k), PTO, workers' comp | No employer-provided benefits |
| **Business expense deductions** | Limited — unreimbursed employee expenses NOT deductible (2018–2025) | Full Schedule C deductions: home office, equipment, mileage, insurance |
| **Control** | Employer controls when, where, and how work is done | Contractor controls schedule, methods, tools, and can work for multiple clients |
| **Tax forms received** | Form W-2 | Form 1099-NEC (if paid $600+ in a calendar year) |
| **Best for** | Stable income, benefits, simpler taxes | Higher earning potential, flexibility, expense deductions |

## The IRS Classification Test

The IRS uses three categories to determine worker status. No single factor is determinative — the totality of the relationship controls.

### Behavioral Control
Does the company control or have the right to control **what** the worker does and **how** they do it? If the company provides training, sets hours, assigns specific methods, or supervises the work in detail — that points toward employee status.

### Financial Control
Does the worker invest in their own equipment and tools? Can they realize a profit or loss? Are they paid a flat fee regardless of time spent? Do they make their services available to other clients? Independent financial investment and multi-client availability point toward contractor status.

### Relationship
Is there a written contract defining the relationship? Are benefits provided (insurance, PTO, retirement)? Is the relationship indefinite or project-based? Is the work a core aspect of the company's regular business? An indefinite relationship performing core functions suggests employment.

## Can I deduct my home office and expenses as a 1099 contractor?

Yes — independent contractors deduct ordinary and necessary business expenses on **Schedule C**. This includes home office expenses (if the space is used regularly and exclusively for business), equipment, supplies, mileage, health insurance premiums, and a portion of self-employment tax. These deductions are NOT available to W-2 employees under current tax law (2018–2025).

---

## Do I have to pay quarterly estimated taxes as a contractor?

If you expect to owe **$1,000 or more** in tax for the year, you're required to make quarterly estimated payments using **Form 1040-ES**. Deadlines are April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15. To avoid underpayment penalties, pay at least 90% of the current year's tax or 100% of last year's tax (110% if AGI exceeds $150,000). This is called the **safe harbor** rule.

---

## What happens if my employer misclassifies me as a contractor?

The employer is liable for back FICA taxes (both the employer AND employee share), federal and state unemployment taxes, and potential penalties. You can file **Form SS-8** with the IRS to request a formal worker classification determination. You may also be entitled to retroactive benefits and overtime pay under the Fair Labor Standards Act. If you suspect misclassification, document everything — the IRS investigates these cases actively.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Reality |
|---|---|
| "If I receive a 1099, I'm legally a contractor" | The form you receive does not determine your legal status — the facts of the working relationship do. The IRS and Department of Labor evaluate the actual arrangement, not the paperwork. |
| "I can deduct my home office as a W-2 employee" | Unreimbursed employee business expenses, including home office deductions, are suspended for W-2 employees under the TCJA (2018–2025). Only independent contractors and certain qualified employees (armed forces reservists, fee-basis officials, performing artists) can deduct unreimbursed expenses. |
| "My employer says I'm a contractor to save them money on payroll taxes" | Employers who knowingly misclassify workers face liability for back taxes, penalties under Section 3509, and in some states, civil penalties per misclassified worker. Intentional misclassification can also trigger Department of Labor wage-and-hour investigations. |
| "I don't need to make quarterly payments if I'm a new contractor" | If you'll owe $1,000+, quarterly estimated taxes are required regardless of how long you've been contracting. The IRS does not waive this for first-year contractors. |
